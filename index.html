The Reduction of Camphor

Sodium borohydride turns the rigid bicyclic ketone camphor into a pair of diastereomeric alcohols — isoborneol (major) and borneol (minor). Which one wins, and why, is a classic lesson in steric approach control. Rotate the real 3D molecules, follow the mechanism, and scale the experiment below.

2 · Why isoborneol wins: steric approach control

NaBH₄ reduction is fast and essentially irreversible, so the product ratio is set by which face the hydride can reach (kinetic control), not by the stability of the products.

1

A roof over the exo face

Camphor is 1,7,7-trimethylbicyclo[2.2.1]heptan-2-one. The two methyl groups on the one-carbon bridge (C7) sit directly over the exo face of the C2 carbonyl, blocking approach from that side.

2

Hydride attacks endo → major

The small hydride (H⁻ from BH₄⁻) takes the open endo face. Adding H to the endo side pushes the new –OH to the exo position, giving isoborneol as the major product.

3

Exo attack → minor

A smaller fraction of hydride pushes past the bridge from the exo face. That sends the –OH endo, giving borneol. Hence the ~90 : 10 preference for isoborneol.

A note on nuance (for the curious)

"Steric approach control" is the picture H. C. Brown introduced using exactly these rigid bicyclic ketones, and it captures the result well. The full modern picture also includes torsional (eclipsing) strain in the transition state and electronic effects; see Wigfield's review for the detailed mechanistic debate. For camphor, the bulky bridge dominates and the outcome is unambiguous.

3 · Step-by-step laboratory procedure

A representative micro/mini-scale protocol (per ~1 g of camphor). Always follow your own lab's safety sheet and your instructor's version — this is an educational summary, not a substitute for a supervised SOP.

  1. Dissolve. Place camphor (~1.0 g, 6.6 mmol) in a flask and dissolve in ~5 mL methanol. Cool in an ice bath.
  2. Add reductant portionwise. Add sodium borohydride (~0.25 g, ≈1 molar equiv = 4 hydride equiv, a deliberate excess) in small portions, swirling. Gas evolution (H₂) is normal — keep flames away and work in a fume hood.
  3. Stir. Remove the ice bath and stir at room temperature (~15–30 min) until the ketone is consumed (TLC, or loss of camphor odor/solid).
  4. Quench. Carefully add water (and dilute acid if your protocol calls for it) to destroy excess NaBH₄ and hydrolyze the borate ester intermediate, releasing the free alcohol.
  5. Isolate. Collect the product — the borneol/isoborneol mixture is a low-melting solid. Filter or extract, wash, and dry per your protocol.
  6. Characterize. Confirm the product and its exo/endo makeup by melting point, IR (loss of C=O ~1740 cm⁻¹, gain of broad O–H ~3300 cm⁻¹), GC, or NMR.
Safety: NaBH₄ releases flammable H₂ and is corrosive; methanol is flammable and toxic. Goggles, gloves, fume hood, no ignition sources. Dispose per institutional guidelines.
