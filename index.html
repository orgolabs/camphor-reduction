The Reduction of Camphor

Sodium borohydride turns the rigid bicyclic ketone camphor into a pair of diastereomeric alcohols — isoborneol (major) and borneol (minor). Which one wins, and why, is a classic lesson in steric approach control. Rotate the real 3D molecules, follow the mechanism, and scale the experiment below.

What is camphor — and what are we making?

Camphor is a terpene originally obtained from the wood of the camphor laurel (Camphora officinarum, native to east and south Asia). You have probably met it as the cooling smell in Vicks VapoRub; over the centuries it has also been used as an insect repellent, a food and perfume additive, and a decongestant in traditional medicine.

Reducing camphor's carbonyl (C=O) group gives two diastereomeric alcohols — isoborneol (exo-OH) and borneol (endo-OH). They share the same connectivity but differ only in how the –OH points on the rigid cage. This experiment uses sodium borohydride (NaBH₄). The more powerful hydride lithium aluminium hydride (LiAlH₄) also reduces camphor, but it must be kept away from water and protic solvents, which makes it trickier and more hazardous — so NaBH₄ is the safer teaching choice.

2 · Why isoborneol wins: steric approach control

NaBH₄ reduction is fast and essentially irreversible, so the product ratio is set by which face the hydride can reach (kinetic control), not by the stability of the products.

Reaction pathway

Hydride adds irreversibly to the open endo face, placing the new –OH exo (isoborneol, major). The initially formed borate ester is hydrolyzed in the aqueous work-up to release the free alcohol.

1

A roof over the exo face

Camphor is 1,7,7-trimethylbicyclo[2.2.1]heptan-2-one. The two methyl groups on the one-carbon bridge (C7) sit directly over the exo face of the C2 carbonyl, blocking approach from that side.

2

Hydride attacks endo → major

The small hydride (H⁻ from BH₄⁻) takes the open endo face. Adding H to the endo side pushes the new –OH to the exo position, giving isoborneol as the major product.

3

Exo attack → minor

A smaller fraction of hydride pushes past the bridge from the exo face. That sends the –OH endo, giving borneol. Hence the ~90 : 10 preference for isoborneol.

How the hydride is delivered (and why water is added at the end)

Borohydride (BH₄⁻) is a hydride shuttle: one NaBH₄ can reduce up to four camphor molecules, handing over one H⁻ at a time. Each transfer builds up a borate ester (the new alkoxide bound to boron). The free alcohol is only released during the aqueous work-up — which is exactly why the procedure adds ice-cold water at the end: it hydrolyzes the borate ester and destroys any leftover NaBH₄.

A note on nuance (for the curious)

"Steric approach control" is the picture H. C. Brown introduced using exactly these rigid bicyclic ketones, and it captures the result well. The full modern picture also includes torsional (eclipsing) strain in the transition state and electronic effects; see Wigfield's review for the detailed mechanistic debate. For camphor, the bulky bridge dominates and the outcome is unambiguous.

3 · Step-by-step laboratory procedure

Reagents & materials (micro scale, per 0.100 g camphor)

ReagentAmountRole
Camphor0.100 g (0.66 mmol)substrate (the ketone)
Methanol0.5 mLprotic solvent
Sodium borohydride, NaBH₄~0.10 g (2.6 mmol, ≈4 molar equiv)hydride source (large excess)
Ice-cold water3.5 mLquench & hydrolyze borate ester
Diethyl ether4.5 mLextraction solvent
Anhydrous MgSO₄spatula tipdrying agent

Procedure

  1. Weigh out 0.100 g camphor and transfer it to a 10-mL Erlenmeyer flask.
  2. In the fume hood, add 0.5 mL methanol to the camphor.
  3. Stir with a glass rod until the camphor has completely dissolved.
  4. Have an ice bath ready.
  5. Weigh about 0.10 g sodium borohydride into a vial.
  6. Cautiously and intermittently add the NaBH₄ to the solution in small portions. It will evolve gas (H₂) and warm up; when it rises above room temperature, return the flask to the ice bath.
  7. Heat the flask on a warm sand bath for ~10 minutes.
  8. Allow the reaction to cool to room temperature.
  9. Carefully add 3.5 mL ice-cold water.
  10. Collect the white solid by vacuum filtration on a Hirsch funnel.
  11. Transfer the solid to a vial.
  12. Add 4.5 mL diethyl ether to dissolve the product, then add anhydrous magnesium sulfate to dry the solution.
  13. Remove the drying agent by transferring the solution into another pre-weighed vial.
  14. Evaporate the ether to leave the product.

Analysis of the alcohol product

  1. Weigh the product and calculate the percentage yield (use the calculator below).
  2. Determine the melting point.
  3. Record the infrared spectrum — look for loss of the C=O band (~1740 cm⁻¹) and a new broad O–H band (~3300 cm⁻¹) confirming the ketone became an alcohol.
Special instructions & safety: All reactants and products are highly volatile — keep them in tightly sealed containers and work in a well-ventilated area or fume hood. Diethyl ether is extremely flammable — no flames or ignition sources nearby. NaBH₄ releases flammable H₂ and is corrosive; methanol is flammable and toxic. Wear goggles and gloves.
Waste disposal: Aqueous solutions from the work-up go in the designated aqueous waste container. Residual methanol or ether goes in the non-halogenated organic waste container.
